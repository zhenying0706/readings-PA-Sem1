# Four Shortlisted GenAI Research Designs for the PhD Proposal

## Final decision

The original ten-design portfolio has been reduced to four candidates. The default three-paper package is **Design 1 + Design 2 + Design 3**. Design 4 replaces Design 3 if access to at least one Singapore public agency and a sufficiently large officer sample is secured.

| New # | Short title | Country | Core method | Portfolio role |
|---:|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | GenAI screening and experienced applicants | Singapore | 2×2 vignette experiment + career-stage heterogeneity + qualitative coding | First qualifying paper |
| 2 | One Government, Unequal Answers? | Singapore | 2×2×2 content audit + framework analysis; optional older-user validation | System/citizen-side paper |
| 3 | Who Bears the Risk? | United States | 2×2 vignette experiment → purposive interviews | Executable third paper |
| 4 | Assistant or Substitute? | Singapore | Pre-interviews → 2×2 experiment → explanatory interviews | Conditional replacement if agency access is secured |

Former Designs 2 and 10 have been consolidated into Design 2's optional user-validation stage. Former Designs 1, 5, 7, and 8 were removed because of weaker fit, excessive access/data risk, or mismatch with the preferred empirical mixed-methods direction.

## 1. Would Generative-AI Screening Deter Experienced Applicants? Administrative Burden and Procedural Safeguards in Singapore Public Service Recruitment

**Advisor theme(s):** Ageism · Mid-career professionals' challenges
**Country:** Singapore only (single-country design; no cross-national comparison)

**Core research question.** In Singapore's job-based public-service recruitment system (Careers@Gov; hiring authority devolved to ministry/statutory-board HR units, with PSC's direct selection role largely confined to scholarship/Administrative Service tracks), would piloting GenAI resume screening for a specific posting deter experienced/mid-late-career applicants more than human screening — and can disclosed procedural safeguards (explanation, human review, appeal channel) buffer that deterrence?

**Anchor literature.** [Keppeler (2024)](../notes/D_AI_and_bureaucratic_work/04-keppeler-2024.md) remains a near-complete disclosure-effect template, but the vignette is now recalibrated to Singapore's actual recruitment architecture rather than a unified-exam model: there is no China-style national civil-service exam; most postings are job-based via Careers@Gov with agency-level screening; VITAL has piloted general AI/ML recruiting tools but GenAI-specific screening is not confirmed as widespread practice. The study is therefore deliberately framed as **anticipatory** research on an emerging, not-yet-established practice — matching the reality that GenAI hiring tools remain pilot-stage almost everywhere.

**Research gap.** Algorithm-aversion-in-recruitment research has not been crossed with age-discrimination/mid-career-employability research; and no study yet tests whether procedural safeguards (explanation, human review, appeal) can buffer AI-screening deterrence for a specific applicant group — a direct, untested extension of [Busuioc (2021)](../notes/E_AI_accountability_legitimacy/01-busuioc-2021.md)'s "explanation/justification" accountability stage into a hiring context. Most AI-hiring-fairness studies also assume a bounded, centralized selection system rather than testing GenAI screening within the decentralized, job-based recruitment architecture that is actually more common.

**Theoretical framework.** Algorithm aversion theory (Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey) explains the base disclosure effect; administrative burden theory (Herd & Moynihan's learning/compliance/psychological cost typology) explains why experienced applicants bear higher burden; Busuioc (2021)'s three-stage accountability framework (information/investigation, explanation/justification, judgment/correction) anchors the procedural-safeguard manipulation — "explanation + human review + appeal channel" maps directly onto the explanation/justification and judgment/correction stages. Causal chain: GenAI-screening disclosure → algorithm aversion and psychological cost, amplified for experienced applicants who already anticipate age bias → lower application willingness/completion; safeguard disclosure → partially offsets this by lowering perceived opacity/unappealability.

**Mixed-methods design.** A single-country (Singapore) vignette experiment built around one specific, realistic Careers@Gov-style job posting (fictional agency, explicitly flagged as a hypothetical pilot scenario to avoid being read as a description of actual current policy). A 2 (screening: GenAI vs. human) × 2 (procedural safeguard: explanation + human review + appeal channel disclosed vs. not) between-subjects factorial, randomized across a sample of working-age Singapore residents/PRs; respondents' actual career stage (early- vs. mid/late-career, by tenure or age band) enters as a measured moderator, yielding a 2×2×2 analytic structure. Quantitative core: application willingness, optional behavioral completion of a mock application form, and an administrative-burden scale (learning/compliance/psychological cost subscales). Embedded qualitative layer: a post-vignette open-text item ("why would/wouldn't you apply?"), coded for "efficiency/neutral" versus "cost-shifting/unfairness" framing.

**Data.** *Existing:* Keppeler (2024)'s published/OSF-deposited vignette wording as the base for adapting the disclosure manipulation (verify archival status and request permission before adapting); publicly viewable Careers@Gov posting formats, used only for ecological validity of the fictional vignette, not as data; the Tripartite Guidelines on Fair Employment Practices and public PSD/VITAL recruitment-policy documentation as institutional-background context. *Original data to collect:* a stratified online panel (Prolific/Cint) of Singapore residents/PRs, stratified by career stage (e.g., <10 vs. ≥15 years' tenure, or corresponding age bands) to ensure adequate cells for both early- and mid/late-career groups. *Enhancement:* a behavioral burden measure — have respondents actually complete a mock Careers@Gov-style application form, recording completion time and drop-off as a behavioral operationalization of "compliance cost" rather than relying on self-report alone.

**Hypotheses.** H1: relative to human screening, disclosed GenAI screening reduces application willingness/completion, more so for mid/late-career than early-career applicants. H2: disclosed procedural safeguards significantly attenuate the H1 effect, with a larger buffering effect for mid/late-career applicants. H3: administrative-burden perceptions (especially the psychological-cost subscale) mediate the effect of GenAI screening × career stage on application willingness. H4 (exploratory): open-text justifications differ systematically by career stage and condition, with "cost-shifting" narratives concentrated among mid/late-career applicants in the no-safeguard condition.

**Contribution.** First systematic bridge between recruitment-context algorithm-aversion research, administrative burden theory, and Busuioc's accountability framework; shifts the debate from "is the exam fair" to a question that actually matches Singapore's institutional reality — whether procedural safeguards offset cost-shifting. As anticipatory research, its evidence is directly usable by PSD/VITAL and similar agencies piloting AI hiring tools, informing safeguard design before large-scale GenAI-screening deployment rather than evaluating it after the fact.

**Feasibility.** High. Single-country, no institutional access required. A 2×2×2 design (with career stage as a measured moderator) targets n≈800–1,000 total (≈100–125/cell) — powered to detect the screening×safeguard interaction and whether it varies by career stage (three-way interaction); pilot on ~100–150 respondents first to check vignette realism and manipulation checks.

**Best next step.** Rewrite Keppeler's published vignette materials into a fictional Careers@Gov-style posting (explicitly flagged as a hypothetical pilot), add the procedural-safeguard disclosure manipulation, confirm feasibility of career-stage-stratified sampling in Singapore via Prolific/Cint, and pilot on ~100–150 respondents before full fielding.

## 2. One Government, Unequal Answers? Auditing Age and Digital-Literacy Responsiveness in Singapore's Public GenAI Assistants

**Advisor theme(s):** Administrative burden · Ageism · Citizen-government relationship
**Country:** Singapore only (single-country design)

**Core research question.** Within Singapore's shared digital-government infrastructure, do public-facing GenAI assistants adapt appropriately to citizens who explicitly signal older age or difficulty navigating digital services, or do they reduce accuracy, completeness, respect, and access to human assistance? How much of the resulting variation occurs within the same system across persona cues, and how much reflects agency-level configuration, language, policy domain, and escalation design?

**Anchor literature.** The correspondence-audit logic in [Bell & Meyer (2024)](../notes/G_Burden_equity_inequality/01-bell-meyer-2024.md) and in the Korea audit design of `03_Performance_Feedback_Causal_Topics_Korea_China_Proposal_Bank.md` (Project 3) supplies the audit methodology, redirected here from human officials to GenAI systems themselves. [Aoki (2025)](../notes/F_Administrative_burden_literacy/05-aoki-2025.md) motivates the human-fallback outcome measure because citizens with digital difficulty particularly value retained human or hybrid channels. [Hegemann & Ritz (2026)](../notes/F_Administrative_burden_literacy/06-hegemann-ritz-2026.md) frames the central mechanism: digital burden can be pre-structured by interface, knowledge-base, clarification, and escalation rules before a citizen meets a human officer. Singapore's VICA infrastructure supplies a substantively important single-country setting because a common whole-of-government platform can still produce agency-specific citizen experiences through local configuration.

**Research gap.** Existing audit experiments in the corpus primarily test human officials' email responsiveness. Research has not established whether public GenAI interfaces provide vulnerability-responsive assistance under a shared national infrastructure, or whether agency configuration and language recreate unequal learning and correction costs. The single-country contribution is therefore not a weaker substitute for a U.S.-Singapore comparison: it identifies where discretion has moved within one centralized digital-government system—from frontline personnel to platform rules, agency knowledge bases, clarification prompts, and human-escalation pathways.

**Mixed-methods design.** First inventory currently accessible Singapore government chatbots and verify which systems genuinely use generative or hybrid AI; do not combine GenAI systems with deterministic FAQ bots. In fresh sessions, submit preregistered matched queries using a factorial design: explicit age cue (older adult versus younger adult), digital-literacy cue (requests step-by-step help versus administratively fluent wording), and language (English versus Chinese, with translation and back-translation). Block randomization by system, service scenario, date, and run so that matched treatments are submitted within a narrow time window despite stochastic outputs and silent model updates. Quantitative analysis scores factual accuracy against contemporaneous official sources, completeness, readability, actionable next steps, unnecessary follow-up effort, clarification quality, respectful tone, and availability of a usable human fallback. Qualitative framework analysis examines empathy, paternalism, unsupported assumptions, redirection, refusal, and escalation. Cross-system comparisons are descriptive unless systems can be tested on the same scenarios; the causal estimands are the randomized within-system differences produced by age and literacy cues.

**Hypotheses.** H1: A well-designed assistant should respond to a low-digital-literacy cue with clearer language, more explicit steps, and more usable human fallback without reducing accuracy or completeness; failure to adapt represents an avoidable learning burden. H2: When age is irrelevant to legal eligibility or procedure, an older-age cue should not reduce factual accuracy, completeness, respect, or access to assistance relative to an otherwise matched younger-age query. H3: Cue effects and baseline response quality will vary across languages and agency implementations even when systems share national infrastructure, revealing where operational discretion resides in configuration rather than in the model label alone.

**Contribution.** Provides the first Singapore-focused, system-level audit of whether GenAI-mediated public-service channels mitigate or reproduce age- and digital-literacy-related administrative burdens. It replaces a broad cross-national comparison with a sharper institutional question: why can citizens receive different levels of clarity, accuracy, and escalation support inside a common whole-of-government AI infrastructure?

**Feasibility.** Moderate to high after a system inventory. The audit requires no participant recruitment, but feasibility depends on identifying at least one clearly generative public concierge and several verifiably hybrid-GenAI agency assistants, confirming their terms of use, and keeping query volume low enough not to disrupt public services. Ethics review should determine whether the study is a non-human-subjects, minimal-risk system audit. Automated readability scores cannot substitute for human validation, and silent system updates require timestamped collection, short matched-query windows, and at least three independent replications.

**Best next step.** Build a live inventory of Singapore's public-facing GenAI systems, beginning with the national digital concierge and VICA-based agency assistants; verify GenAI status and allowed research access before naming the final cases. Pilot a balanced 2×2×2 design on two systems and four age-neutral service scenarios, run each cell three times in a fresh session, validate the scoring rubric with two independent coders, and calculate inter-rater reliability before preregistering the full audit.

## 3. Who Bears the Risk? Age-Equity Concerns, Procedural Safeguards, and U.S. Public Employees' Willingness to Use GenAI

**Advisor theme(s):** Administrative burden · Ageism · Citizen-government relationship  
**Country:** United States only (single-country design; no Singapore-U.S. comparison)

**Core research question.** When a GenAI case-processing tool may impose disproportionate error and correction burdens on older beneficiaries, does that equity risk reduce U.S. public employees' willingness to deploy it, and can explanation, human review, appeal, and correction safeguards restore support?

**Anchor literature.** [Nam & Bell (2025)](../notes/B_GenAI-specific/06-nam-bell-2025.md) supplies the public-values framing experiment. [Busuioc (2021)](../notes/E_AI_accountability_legitimacy/01-busuioc-2021.md) supplies the information, judgment, and consequences stages of accountability. The administrative-burden literature supplies the citizen-side outcomes: GenAI may lower organizational processing costs while shifting learning, verification, correction, and appeal costs to beneficiaries.

**Research gap.** Existing work shows that equity-risk framing can suppress bureaucratic support for AI, but does not establish whether age-based vulnerability functions in the same way or whether concrete procedural safeguards can restore support. The contribution is therefore not another generic trust study; it tests whether institutional design changes the efficiency-equity trade-off.

**Mixed-methods design.** Use an explanatory sequential QUAN→QUAL design. After 5–8 cognitive pretests, field a 2×2 vignette experiment among screened U.S. state and local public employees involved in service delivery or program administration: age-equity risk absent/present × procedural safeguards absent/present. Safeguards include a meaningful explanation, human final review, an appeal channel, correction of errors, and monitoring of age-group error rates. Primary outcomes are deployment recommendation, budget allocation, proportion of caseload delegated to GenAI, demand for human review/audit, perceived fairness, and expected administrative burden. Invite prior survey participants to follow-up interviews and purposively select approximately 20–25 contrasting response profiles.

**Sampling and verification.** Use a paid two-stage occupational screener and non-identifying consistency checks for government level, role family, tenure, and service-delivery responsibilities. Treat the resulting sample as a screened nonprobability sample, not a nationally representative or officially verified sample. Do not request agency documents, badges, work email, or confidential information.

**Hypotheses.** H1: age-equity-risk information reduces deployment support and GenAI task delegation. H2: procedural safeguards increase support and reduce expected citizen correction/appeal burdens. H3: safeguards have their largest buffering effect when age-equity risk is salient. H4: follow-up interviews explain why safeguards reassure some employees but fail for others, including workload, symbolic-compliance, professional-discretion, and accountability concerns.

**Identification and evidence boundary.** Random assignment identifies the effect of the vignette conditions on stated allocation and deployment decisions. It does not identify actual agency adoption, real service outcomes, or a causal mediation effect through perceived fairness. Interviews provide mechanism-consistent explanations, not independent causal proof.

**Feasibility.** Moderate to high. The study can be completed remotely from Singapore. Plan provisionally for approximately 450–650 survey respondents, subject to pilot-based power analysis, plus 20–25 interviews. The principal limitation is occupational verification and nonprobability sampling, not geography.

**Best next step.** Adapt the Nam and Bell vignette into one realistic state/local public-service context, preregister the 2×2 design and primary outcomes, pilot the screener and manipulation, and use the pilot to set the final sample size and interview-selection rules.

## 4. Assistant or Substitute? Career Stage, Professional Identity Threat, and GenAI Use in the Singapore Public Service

**Advisor theme(s):** Mid-career professionals' challenges
**Country:** Singapore only (single-country design; no cross-national comparison)

**Core research question.** When Singapore public-sector organizations frame GenAI as augmenting officers' professional judgment versus as a tool for standardization and headcount reduction, does this shape professional identity threat, task delegation, and responsible use — and does the effect vary by career stage, professional experience, and organizational role?

**Anchor literature and real-world backdrop.** [Kim & Nam (2026)](../notes/B_GenAI-specific/04-kim-nam-2026.md) — Prof. Kim's own conditional-trust model of officials' GenAI use — remains the direct theoretical anchor and explicitly calls for task-level, longitudinal, and cross-country extensions. Unlike the original version of this design, it no longer needs to hypothesize a future large-scale GenAI rollout: Singapore's MDDI stated in a November 2025 parliamentary reply that roughly 80% of ~150,000 public-sector staff have used the government's internal GenAI tool, Pair, alongside a mandatory AI-literacy curriculum for all public officers. GovTech positions Pair explicitly as an assistive tool (writing, research, analysis, summarization, custom-assistant creation) that requires staff to check outputs and retain responsibility. This gives the study a real, large-scale, ongoing transformation rather than a hypothetical future one — but current public documentation only establishes that Pair is widely used, not that it is substituting for officers' final decisions, so any substitution/monitoring/automated-decision content must be framed explicitly as a hypothetical vignette condition, and Pair is deliberately kept out of the title to avoid implying confirmed research access to Pair users or its usage data.

**Research gap.** No paper in the corpus treats tenure/career stage as a theoretically motivated moderator of GenAI response. More importantly, existing literature — including Kim & Nam's own study — largely examines willingness to adopt in early/hypothetical contexts, not how an organization's framing of an already-real, large-scale GenAI transformation (as empowering versus as a cost/standardization tool) causally shapes identity threat and task delegation. This is exactly the empirical opportunity Singapore's current situation offers that most other jurisdictions cannot yet.

**Theoretical framework.** Kim & Nam's conditional-trust model, extended with occupational identity threat theory (Petriglieri 2011) and career-stage theory (Super; Ng & Feldman), plus two new threads: a framing/loss-framing mechanism (the same logic as [Nam & Bell (2025)](../notes/B_GenAI-specific/06-nam-bell-2025.md)'s public-values framing used in Design #3) explaining why "empowerment vs. efficiency-substitution" framing shifts identity threat; and "professional control safeguards" mapped onto [Busuioc (2021)](../notes/E_AI_accountability_legitimacy/01-busuioc-2021.md)'s judgment/correction accountability stage — the same theoretical anchor as Design #1's procedural safeguards, giving the two designs a shared methodological signature. Causal chain: organizational framing → identity threat → delegation willingness/responsible use; safeguards → buffer identity threat independent of framing; career stage/tenure/role → moderates effect size.

**Mixed-methods design (three phases).** *Phase 1 (qual, pre-interviews):* 8–12 Singapore public-sector employees, mapping actual GenAI use, core professional tasks, assistant-vs-substitute perceptions, skill/promotion concerns, and career-stage self-understanding — informs instrument design only, no theory-testing role. *Phase 2 (quant, 2×2 vignette experiment):* all participants read a scenario expanding internal GenAI use to resident-response drafting, policy summarization, and preliminary case opinions; randomized on organizational framing (professional-empowerment vs. efficiency-substitution) × professional-control safeguards (present: staff involved in design, trained, retain final decision, can override AI recommendations vs. absent: system designed top-down, usage monitored, deviating from AI recommendations requires extra justification). Career stage/tenure/role type enters as a measured moderator, not a manipulated one — no causal claim about career stage itself, only about whether treatment effects vary by it. *Phase 3 (qual, explanatory interviews):* 20–25 purposively sampled interviews across identity-threat levels, framing conditions, and adoption/resistance patterns, spanning frontline, policy-analysis, and managerial roles — explaining why the same information produces divergent reactions, what expertise officers see as irreplaceable, whether retained final-decision authority suffices to lower threat, and how culture/supervisors/performance systems shape real use.

**Data.** *Existing:* Kim & Nam's own 2023 KIPA survey (1,608 Korean officials) — used as an instrument-adaptation template (trust, operational-concern, supportive-culture items), not as Singapore data itself; still worth asking Prof. Kim directly about reuse for instrument design. Public Pair background documents (GovTech's Pair introduction, Pair terms of use, MDDI's November 2025 parliamentary reply) as institutional-context and vignette-realism sources, not data per se. *Original data to collect, by feasibility tier:* **Best tier** — formal partnership with 2+ agencies (e.g., GovTech, PSD, Civil Service College, or a ministry/statutory board), n≈400–600, vignette experiment + anonymized usage logs (opt-in status, frequency/persistence, feature range, pre/post-training change — no work content) + interviews; depends on formal partnership, not assumed achievable now. **Workable tier** — partnership with one larger ministry or statutory board, n≈250–400, survey experiment + interviews; publishable, but with limited external validity. **Fallback tier** — commercial-panel-screened (Prolific/Cint) Singapore public-sector employees, n≈150–250; suitable as a pilot, not representative of the Singapore public service as a whole; strictly define "Singapore public officer," excluding teachers, hospital staff, SOE employees, government contractors, and general private-sector respondents. **Ideal enhancement** (requires formal partnership, not assumed achievable) — randomized training/implementation messaging → 6–8 weeks of real usage-log observation → follow-up survey → purposive interviews, upgrading the outcome from stated willingness to actual use behavior.

**Decision rule.** Spend 2–4 weeks confirming access to at least one agency and roughly how many respondents are reachable. If 250+ respondents are reachable with follow-up-interview access, proceed with this Singapore design as a primary paper. If access can't be confirmed in that window, keep the design on file, lead with Design #1 as the first qualifying paper, and treat this design as a pilot or second paper.

**Ethical & sensitivity safeguards.** Singapore public-sector employees may worry that honest answers could affect their standing at work, so identity threat and resistance are easily underreported unless the design explicitly: avoids routing responses through direct supervisors; does not require an institutional email as research data; asks nothing about classified projects, real cases, or internal documents; collects no actual Pair chat content; never reports results for units/roles with very small cell sizes back to the agency; keeps interview quotes fully anonymized; and states plainly to respondents that results will not feed into individual performance review.

**Hypotheses.** H1: the efficiency-substitution framing elicits higher professional identity threat than the professional-empowerment framing. H2: professional-control safeguards (retained final decision, ability to override) attenuate identity threat and increase delegation willingness/responsible use, independent of framing. H3: framing and safeguard effects vary by career stage/tenure, with mid-career officers showing the largest framing-driven swing and the largest safeguard-driven buffering. H4 (exploratory/qual): explanatory interviews reveal why the same condition produces divergent reactions, what expertise officers see as irreplaceable, whether retained final-decision authority suffices to lower threat, and how culture/supervisors/performance systems shape real use.

**Contribution.** The only design in this set that embeds theory in a real, large-scale, ongoing GenAI transformation rather than a hypothetical future scenario; extends Kim & Nam (2026) exactly along the task-level direction they call for; shows that how GenAI is implemented (framing + safeguards), not merely whether it exists, causally drives identity threat and delegation — directly usable for GovTech/PSD/CSC training and safeguard design; shares a theoretical anchor with Design #1, giving the portfolio a coherent methodological throughline.

**Best next step.** Draft the Phase 1 pre-interview protocol (8–12 respondents), spend 2–4 weeks confirming at least one agency contact and rough recruitable headcount, and apply the decision rule to choose between fielding this design as a primary paper or parking it as a pilot/second paper; in parallel, draft the vignette materials (framing × safeguard manipulation) and the identity-threat/delegation scale items.
